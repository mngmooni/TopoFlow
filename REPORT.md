# 1. Introduction

Robot motion planning requires finding trajectories that connect a start configuration to a goal while satisfying geometric constraints imposed by obstacles. In environments containing multiple obstacles, however, there may be several qualitatively different ways to reach the same destination. These alternatives can belong to distinct *homotopy classes*, representing paths that cannot be continuously deformed into one another without crossing an obstacle. Consequently, a useful generative planner should not only produce collision-free trajectories but also represent the diversity of available routes and allow control over which route is generated.

Recent advances in generative modeling have made it possible to learn multimodal distributions over trajectories. Diffusion-based motion planners, for example, can capture diverse solutions from previously generated trajectories (Carvalho et al., 2023). Flow Matching (FM) provides an alternative approach by learning continuous-time vector fields that transport samples from a simple prior distribution toward a target distribution (Lipman et al., 2023). Despite these capabilities, applying generative models to obstacle-constrained planning presents two related but distinct challenges. First, a model must distinguish between different topological route classes. Second, even when the intended route class is correctly specified, the generated trajectory may still violate geometric constraints. **Topological consistency does not, by itself, guarantee collision avoidance.**

Existing approaches address these challenges through several mechanisms, including topology-based candidate selection, constraint guidance, and optimization-based projection during generation. Topology-aware diffusion methods can generate and evaluate candidates across different route classes, while projected generative methods attempt to enforce feasibility through constrained optimization (Christopher et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2026). These strategies provide important mechanisms for constrained planning, but they also motivate a complementary question: **Can the feasible trajectory representation itself be structured so that generated paths satisfy the desired route and geometric constraints without repeated inference-time correction?**

In this work, we investigate this question through **Topology-Aware Flow Matching (TDFM)**, a generative trajectory planning framework that combines explicit route-class conditioning with class-specific feasible representations. We study a family of two-dimensional, x-monotone paths among static obstacles. A discrete route code specifies whether the path passes above or below each obstacle, and a class-conditioned representation is constructed from intervals of collision-free waypoint positions. These intervals define a feasible chart in which a continuous latent representation can be decoded into a piecewise-linear trajectory. Rather than generating unconstrained waypoint coordinates and subsequently correcting them, TDFM learns a conditional flow directly in the latent space of the feasible chart. A separate topology selector provides a mechanism for choosing among available route classes.

This construction separates three aspects of generative planning: selecting a route class, ensuring geometric feasibility within that class, and learning a distribution of trajectories within the feasible region. Under explicit assumptions on the obstacle geometry, waypoint grid, and chart construction, every decoded trajectory from a feasible chart satisfies a prescribed obstacle-clearance constraint. Under additional conditions ensuring valid topological cuts, the decoded trajectory also realizes the specified reduced crossing word. The guarantee therefore arises from the representation and its geometric properties rather than from an assumption that the learned vector field is perfectly accurate.

We evaluate TDFM through a sequence of controlled experiments examining topological adherence, geometric validity, class coverage, generative diversity, and computational efficiency. Our results show that route-conditioned Flow Matching alone can maintain strong route adherence while still producing geometrically invalid trajectories. In contrast, chart-based generation and chart-aware projection both satisfy the tested geometric constraints, allowing us to isolate the additional effect of learning directly in chart coordinates. Compared with an adapted projection-based baseline operating on the same feasible charts, TDFM retains greater within-class diversity at lower inference cost, whereas projection produces shorter, smoother trajectories with larger obstacle clearance. Class-matched comparisons with a sampling-planner reference further reveal that TDFM better preserves the reference trajectory spread but tends to generate paths closer to obstacles.

The main contributions of this work are:

1. **Topology-conditioned generation in feasible charts.** We integrate explicit route-class representations with Flow Matching in a class-specific feasible trajectory space, and establish conditional geometric and topological validity statements for the decoded paths.

2. **A controlled analysis of feasibility, diversity, and computation.** We distinguish the effect of topology conditioning from that of feasibility-preserving representations and compare direct chart-space generation with chart-aware inference-time projection using matched trajectories and evaluation budgets.

3. **A class-coverage and feasibility audit.** We develop a chart-based procedure for analyzing the feasibility of candidate route classes, distinguishing certified infeasibility from failures of a sampling-based reference planner to discover feasible trajectories.

The present study focuses on static two-dimensional environments with circular and axis-aligned rectangular obstacles and an x-monotone trajectory family. Our findings characterize the capabilities and limitations of topology-aware generative planning within this setting, without claiming guarantees for arbitrary homotopy classes, robot dynamics, or continuous trajectory tracking.
